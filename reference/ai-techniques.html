The Techniques

Six ideas behind the demonstrations.

01Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)

Lewis et al., 2020 · class Sessions 11–12

The idea

A language model on its own answers from what it learned in training. It may be out of date, and it may invent details. RAG adds a step: before answering, the system retrieves relevant documents from an index, adds them to the model’s context, and then generates an answer that points to those sources.

How it came up

The Perplexity case described three parts working together: a reformulator that clarifies what the user really asked, an index that ranks credible sources above weaker ones, and RAG itself, which produces cited answers. The professor’s point was that this reduces hallucination and also reduces the need for expert prompting, because the reformulator tidies the question first.

Why it matters

RAG is the same idea as attaching documents to a custom GPT: the answer rests on your own material. For a company, it is the practical route to a tool that answers from its manuals or policies. Its limits are that the answer is only as good as the sources retrieved, and a source can still be misread.

Illustration

A product-guide assistant that retrieves the right section of a tooling catalogue before answering is using retrieval. A plain chatbot asked the same question would answer from general knowledge and could quote the wrong specification.

02Context design for custom GPTs and Gems

Class framework · Sessions 9–10

The idea

“Context” is what you tell the custom tool before any user speaks to it. It is what separates a purposeful tool from a generic chatbot. The class exercise was to build a tool that takes a business problem and recommends a framework, not a solution. The notes suggest five layers:

LayerWhat it saysExample
RoleWhat the tool is and doesYou are a management framework advisor.
Knowledge baseThe frameworks it knows: name, origin, what it addresses, when it applies and when notTeece, 1986: who captures the value of an innovation.
Decision logicHow to map a problem to a frameworkSpot the theme, match it, explain priority if several apply.
Output formatExactly how to answerFramework name, one paragraph of reasoning, two or three guiding questions.
GuardrailsWhat it must avoidNo direct solutions; no framework without reasons; no default to a single framework.

A weak and a strong instruction

  • Weak: “Help managers with strategy.” It invites generic, impulsive answers.
  • Strong: “When given a business problem, name the single most suitable framework, explain why in one paragraph, ask two guiding questions, and never propose a solution.”

Why it matters

The quality of the tool is bounded by the quality of the context. The exercise also tested the student’s understanding: you cannot write the decision logic without knowing when each framework applies. See the frameworks page for the lenses suggested in class.

03Custom GPTs and Gems versus Agentic AI

Session 7–8 clarification

The idea

A custom GPT or Gem gives customised answers. It may use your documents, a persona and rules. It does not take actions in the world, such as sending an email or booking a meeting. Tools that do take actions on a user’s behalf belong to Agentic AI, which was announced as a later topic. In the innovation pattern, Agentic AI is the next stage of the same product, not a separate category (see Pattern of Industrial Innovation).

Custom GPT or GemAgentic AI
OutputAnswers and draftsAnswers and actions
AccessDocuments and instructions you provideAlso calendars, email and other systems, where allowed
Risk focusWrong or invented answersWrong actions, so approvals and limits matter more

Practical limits mentioned in class: a paid plan was needed for Custom GPTs while Gems worked on a free account; shared links needed the recipient to have an account; both were being migrated to new formats. Check current terms before relying on any of these.

04Human-in-the-loop design

Session 7–8 decision for Delta Core

The idea

Deciding where a person sits relative to the AI. The class decision for Delta Core was “AI as co-pilot, not a replacement”: automate high-volume, low-risk, repetitive work first, and keep human validation for client-facing strategic recommendations. A simple way to place a task is by two questions: how costly is a mistake, and how easily can it be undone?

Task typePlacementWhy
Report generation, dashboards, A/B testing, first draftsAutomateRepetitive, low risk, easy to correct
Analysis that a client will rely onAugmentAI drafts and finds patterns; a person checks and signs off
Reading emotional or contextual signals; strategic adviceHuman decidesTacit knowledge and judgement; the class example was a throwaway remark that analytics missed

The proportion of human involvement as AI improves was recorded in class as the central unanswered question. This table is my way of organising the decision, not a framework from the professor.

05The prompt improvement loop

Demonstration · Sessions 11–12

The idea

Instead of writing a perfect prompt in one go, draft it, ask the tool to improve it, then read the improved version line by line before running it. The professor stressed the reading step: blindly copying a refined prompt hides what you are asking for.

  • Draft a clear first prompt with the purpose, the audience and the frameworks to use.
  • Ask the tool to critique and improve it.
  • Read every line; remove or fix anything that does not match your intent.
  • Run it, check the output against your sources, and repeat if needed.

Why it matters

It saves time while keeping you accountable for what was asked. It connects to the best-practice habits on the ChatGPT best-practices page (structured prompting and checking outputs critically).

06From proof of concept to scale

Raised by students in Sessions 11–12 · my framing, not from class

The idea

Students asked how to move from a proof of concept (POC) to a working solution and how to judge the return on AI investment. The course has not yet covered it. This block is my own outline, drawing on general project practice, and is labelled as such.

StageQuestion to answer before moving on
Proof of conceptDoes it work technically on a small, real sample?
PilotDo real users accept it, and does it save time or improve quality in measurable terms?
Minimum viable productCan it run reliably with proper data access, security and ownership?
ScaleIs the benefit larger than the full cost, including licences, support and training?

Questions raised by students on governance, such as cost per prompt, oversight by IT and HR, and organisation-wide licences, belong at the MVP and scale gates. For a formal risk approach, see NIST AI Risk Management Framework and the EU AI Act.
