The Frameworks

Seven lenses from the live sessions.

01Complementary Assets Framework

David J. Teece · Research Policy, 1986 · class Sessions 9–10 and 11–12

The idea

An innovation does not earn its inventor money on its own. To turn it into a product that customers can buy, a firm needs complementary assets: manufacturing, distribution, service, brand, and, for Generative AI, cloud computing. Teece argued that when the idea is easy to copy, value flows to whoever controls these assets, not necessarily to whoever invented the technology. The firm has two options: build the assets, or borrow them through partnerships.

How it was used in class

  • Google and Gen AI: Google published the transformer architecture but did not lead the market early. The framework points to execution and product design as the gap, because Google already owns cloud, custom chips and an ecosystem.
  • OpenAI: it lacked its own cloud and closed the gap through a partnership with a cloud provider.
  • Perplexity: it has no cloud infrastructure of its own, so competing head-on with the cloud owners as a general-purpose tool is hard. This was the first step in the professor’s derivation.

Why it matters

It stops a manager from praising an invention and forgetting the rest of the chain. Before investing in an AI capability, ask who would capture its value and what else we need to own or rent for that to be us. It also explains why partnerships between a start-up and a platform owner are common.

Illustration

A new tool coating is only worth what the supplier can deliver through application engineering, distribution and customer service. A competitor with a larger service network could capture most of the value of a similar coating. Mapping which of these assets we hold shows where to build and where to partner.

02Pattern of Industrial Innovation

William J. Abernathy and James M. Utterback · Technology Review, 1978 · class Sessions 9–10

The idea

Innovation in an industry follows a pattern. In the fluid phase, many product designs compete and products change fast. A dominant design then emerges and the industry enters a transitional phase. In the specific phase, product changes are small and the competition moves to process: cost, quality and scale.

How it was used in class

The professor placed Generative AI in the fluid phase: features change about every six months and no dominant design has emerged. The light bulb was the analogy: filament, tungsten, fluorescent, then LED, with each step won by whoever tracked society’s changing needs fastest. Agentic AI was described as the next step on the same path, not a separate product. Explainable AI showed user demand steering a product feature.

Why it matters

The right strategy depends on the phase. In a fluid phase, flexibility and speed of learning win and heavy process investment is risky. In a specific phase, cost and process discipline win. Betting on process efficiency too early can lock a firm into a design that later loses.

Illustration

Machining-process monitoring is still fluid in many plants: sensors, data formats and software differ widely. A manager would pilot flexibly rather than standardise one vendor across all sites. Cutting inserts are mature, so competition there is on process and cost.

03Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning (STP)

Wendell Smith (1956) for segmentation; popularised by Philip Kotler · class Sessions 11–12

The idea

Segmentation divides a market into groups with different needs. Targeting chooses the groups worth serving. Positioning decides how the offer should be seen by that group compared with alternatives. STP comes before decisions about features, price and channel because those depend on who the customer is.

How it was used in class

In the Perplexity case the class jumped to features such as deep search, an enterprise version and voice search. The professor’s corrected order was: complementary assets show Perplexity must be a niche player; STP then picks the segment (for example researchers, financial analysts or medical professionals); and only then do we choose which feature serves that segment best.

Why it matters

A feature is only good or bad for a particular customer. Choosing features first gives a list of opinions. Choosing the segment first gives a test for every option.

Illustration

For a machining-analytics service, segments might be high-volume automotive plants, aerospace job shops and small contract machinists. Their needs on speed, accuracy and support differ, so a feature that suits one may waste money in another.

04Two-directional AI strategy and the micro-productivity trap

Class framework · Sessions 7–8 and 9–10

The idea

AI strategy has to move in two directions at once.

  • Bottom-up: find where AI improves existing offerings and processes. This is necessary but not enough.
  • Top-down: a CXO-level question: could AI reinvent the business model, or make ours obsolete?

The micro-productivity trap

The professor’s warning was that focusing only on small gains is a mindset lock-in, which comes in two forms:

  • Offering lock-in: thinking only about how AI can improve what the firm sells today.
  • Process lock-in: thinking only about how AI can improve how the firm works today.

The firm may improve efficiently while a competitor changes the product itself. The comparison made was with digital transformation: industries close to digital products, such as text and music, were rebuilt, while construction and agriculture barely changed. The question is which services are closest to becoming “AI products”.

How it was used in class

For Delta Core Analytics, report automation and dashboards are bottom-up gains. The top-down question is what happens if rivals offer AI-driven marketing analytics as a ready-made product. A classmate’s example from vehicle development, replacing physical prototype tests with end-to-end virtual verification, was treated as value-chain thinking rather than a process tweak. Frontline staff often spot AI opportunities before management does, so the two directions should meet.

Illustration

Bottom-up: an assistant that drafts customer quotations faster. Top-down: asking whether customers will one day request machining solutions from a self-service AI platform that bypasses application engineers altogether.

05Core asset and core activity threat lens

Presented in class · Sessions 9–10 · original source to be confirmed

The idea

Ask two questions about any firm facing AI: is a core asset threatened (for example data, brand or installed base), and is a core activity threatened (for example the work the firm does to create value)? The answer suggests a strategic response. If neither is threatened, use AI to improve. If one is threatened, adapt around the one that remains. If both are threatened, the logic discussed in class was “harvest and exit”: take the remaining profit and leave rather than invest into decline.

SafeSafeImprove with AI (bottom-up)

The table is my summary of the logic described in class. The notes do not name an original author for this lens, so I have not attributed it. I will add the source once I confirm it with the professor.

Why it matters

It forces a clear classification before spending on AI. It also makes “exit” a legitimate option, which many transformation plans avoid mentioning.

06Transition versus transformation

Distinction clarified by the professor · Sessions 7–8

The idea

Transition uses technology to improve existing offerings or processes. Transformation disrupts the value chain so that how value is added changes fundamentally. The professor linked this to the value chain: a firm buys low-value inputs, adds value and sells at a higher price, and the job of AI is to widen that gap, ideally by finding new value and not only by speeding up the old one.

  • Transition example: an AI tool that shortens the time to produce a technical report.
  • Transformation example: replacing the report with a continuously updated recommendation delivered to the customer’s machine.

Why it matters

Most managers stop at transition because it is easier to measure. Naming the difference makes the choice visible and ties directly to the micro-productivity trap. For a business transformation career, this is the central distinction. For the broader value chain concept, see Value Chain on the Beyond the Toolkit page.

07The derivation sequence

Professor’s method · Sessions 9–10 and 11–12

The idea

Four steps for any case answer: state the problem, list the assumptions, apply the framework logic step by step, and derive the conclusion. The professor contrasted deriving a conclusion with justifying one already decided.

Worked example: a price cut for Gemini

  • Problem: Gemini has fewer users than ChatGPT.
  • Assumptions: users choose on perceived value; cost is not the only barrier.
  • Framework logic: complementary assets show Google holds cloud and chips, so the gap is product and execution; the innovation pattern shows a fluid market where features matter more than price.
  • Conclusion: a price cut alone is not justified. The cheapest option was used by almost no one in a class of about 70, so price is relative to value.

Where AI tools fit

The professor asked that AI tools be used to find relevant frameworks, not to skip the thinking. In class, a custom GPT suggested Blue Ocean Strategy for the Perplexity case (see Blue Ocean Strategy). To build such a tool yourself, see context design on the AI techniques page.
