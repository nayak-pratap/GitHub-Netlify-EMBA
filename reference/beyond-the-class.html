Extra Reading

Four lenses that extend the class discussions.

01Disruptive innovation

Clayton M. Christensen · The Innovator’s Dilemma, 1997 · Christensen and Raynor, 2003

The idea

Established firms serve their best customers with better and better products. A newcomer begins with a simpler, cheaper product that the best customers do not want, wins a low-end or new market, improves, and then moves up. The incumbent, focused on its most profitable customers, responds too late. Not every new entrant is disruptive; the theory is specific about starting in overlooked or non-consuming segments.

Link to class

In Delta Core Analytics, rivals using Generative AI delivered insights faster and about 40% cheaper while clients questioned the firm’s slower approach. Whether those rivals are truly disruptive or simply better is a question the theory helps to ask: do they serve overlooked customers first, and are they improving fast enough to move up?

Why it matters

It explains why leaders who do everything “right” can still lose. It supports the top-down direction on the two-directional strategy.

02Jobs to be Done

Christensen, Hall, Dillon and Duncan · Harvard Business Review, 2016 · building on Anthony Ulwick’s work

The idea

Customers “hire” a product to make progress in a particular situation. Instead of describing the customer by age or industry, describe the job: what are they trying to get done, and what are they using now? Different customer types can hire the same product for different jobs, and the same customer can hire different products for the same job.

Link to class

It gives a sharper basis for the niche choice in the Perplexity derivation than demographics alone. A researcher hiring Perplexity to “find a citable answer quickly” has a different job from a casual user who wants a general answer, and the job points to the feature that matters. It can sit alongside STP.

Illustration

A machinist might hire an application engineer’s recommendation to “avoid a scrapped part on the first run”, not to “learn about cutting tools”. The job suggests what to build: fast, trusted guidance at the machine.

03Ansoff Matrix

H. Igor Ansoff · Harvard Business Review, 1957

The idea

Growth comes from four paths, on two axes: new or existing products and new or existing markets. Market penetration is lowest risk. Product development and market development change one dimension. Diversification changes both and carries the highest risk.

Link to class

It gives structure to the offering lock-in and process lock-in warning. Improving an existing offering for existing clients is penetration. A new AI-based offering is product development. Applying it to a new type of client is diversification. Seeing which box an idea sits in helps the team decide how much risk it is taking.

04EU AI Act: risk tiers

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 · overview only, not legal advice

The idea

The EU AI Act regulates AI systems by risk. Practices judged to threaten fundamental rights are prohibited. High-risk systems, such as AI used in certain safety, employment or essential-service decisions, must meet requirements on risk management, data quality, documentation, human oversight and accuracy. Limited-risk systems such as chatbots mainly face transparency duties. Most other systems are minimal risk with few obligations. Obligations come into force in stages, so the current dates should be checked.

Link to class

Delta Core’s decision to set up data governance and compliance checks before scaling AI, and students’ questions on oversight, are the practical side of this regime. It pairs with the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, which is voluntary and process-oriented, whereas the Act is law for firms covered by it.

Why it matters

A manager does not need to master the text. The useful habit is to classify an AI use by risk early, because the risk class decides how much documentation and human oversight it will need. For legal conclusions, a qualified adviser should be consulted.
