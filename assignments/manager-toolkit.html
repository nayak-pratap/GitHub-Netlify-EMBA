1 · Six Sigma DMAIC

A. Explanation of the framework

Origin. Six Sigma began at Motorola in the mid-1980s, associated with engineer Bill Smith and later developed by Mikel Harry. General Electric's adoption in the mid-1990s made it a mainstream management method (Harry & Schroeder, 2000). DMAIC is its improvement method for existing processes.

Core idea. Every process produces variation, and variation produces defects, delay and cost. Six Sigma asks a manager to stop arguing from opinion and instead measure the process, find the few causes that drive most of the problem, fix those, and lock the gain in. The famous target of 3.4 defects per million opportunities is a statement of how little variation a process should show relative to what the customer will accept.

  1. 1Define — state the problem, the customer requirement, the scope and the goal in a short project charter
  2. 2Measure — decide what to measure, check the measurement can be trusted, and record the baseline
  3. 3Analyze — use Pareto charts, cause-and-effect diagrams and the 5 Whys to find the root causes the data supports, not the ones people assume
  4. 4Improve — generate solutions for those root causes, pilot them, and confirm the metric actually moves
  5. 5Control — make the gain permanent with standard work, control charts, clear ownership and a response plan if the process drifts

B. Why it is one of the most important frameworks for a manager

Problems it solves. Rework, long and unpredictable lead times, inconsistent quality, and problems that are "fixed" again and again. At the frontline it gives teams a method for cutting defects. Middle managers use it to sponsor projects and hold people to evidence. Senior leaders use it to decide which processes deserve attention.

What a manager misses without it. Improvement tends to start at the solution: a new tool, system or extra headcount bought before anyone knows the baseline or the root cause. Gains are then hard to prove, and they decay because nobody owns the Control phase.

Limitations. DMAIC works best on repeatable processes with measurable output and enough data. It is heavy for small, obvious fixes, and a poor fit for one-off or creative decisions such as entering a new market, where there is no process to measure. Applied rigidly it becomes tool-driven paperwork and discourages experimentation. Its rigour also depends on trustworthy data: a poor measurement system produces confident but wrong conclusions.

Why it earns its place. For a manager moving from technical to business leadership, DMAIC is also a shared language. It lets you tell a finance leader or a customer exactly what changed, by how much, and how you know, which turns engineering instinct into evidence other functions accept.

C. Real-life managerial case

Contributor: Pratap Nayak · Reducing engineering rework and lead time

  1. 1Context — a global cutting-tool manufacturer's captive engineering centre that prepares customer-specific tooling solutions. I was the manager of the engineering team.
  2. 2The challenge — engineering work was being revised repeatedly, consuming many hours, and solution delivery took about 8 days. The decision was how to cut revision effort and lead time without hurting quality, with customer responsiveness at stake.
  3. 3What happened — we ran process optimisation initiatives that combined automation with continuous-improvement practice. Revision hours fell by about 60%, worth roughly USD 150,000 a year, and lead time reached a record 5 days, down from 8.
  4. 4Applying the framework — read back through DMAIC. Define: the customer need is a faster, right-first-time solution, so lead time and revision hours become the measures. Measure: baseline both across projects, using the project data we already held. Analyze: rank the causes of revision with a Pareto chart, and ask the 5 Whys on the top few before automating anything. Improve: automate or standardise only the steps the analysis points to, and pilot first. Control: track the two measures on a dashboard with an owner and a trigger for action, such as a control chart that flags drift before customers feel it.
  5. 5What would change — a formal Define-and-Measure step would give a documented baseline, so the 60% and 8-to-5-day results are provable rather than remembered. A Pareto-first Analyze phase would also guard against automating a step that is visible but not the largest cause. A written control plan would make it clear who owns the numbers after the improvement effort moves on.
  6. 6Lesson — measure before you automate. A baseline turns an improvement story into evidence, and a control plan keeps it from fading. The same discipline is what I would bring to improvement work in functions beyond engineering.

D. A published Indian example

Tata Steel is documented as applying a DMAIC-based approach, which it called ASPIRE, to process improvement: Deshpande, Halder and Kumar (2005), “Process improvement at Tata Steel using the ASPIRE DMAIC approach”, in World Class Applications of Six Sigma, pp. 45–61. I cite it as a published example of an Indian organisation using a DMAIC-based method in a large process industry. I have not reproduced its project details or results here.

2 · Kotter's 8 Steps for Leading Change

A. Explanation of the framework

Origin. John P. Kotter of Harvard Business School published the model in his 1995 Harvard Business Review article "Leading Change: Why Transformation Efforts Fail", and expanded it in the 1996 book Leading Change. It grew out of his observation of more than a hundred companies trying to transform themselves (Kotter, 1995; 1996).

Core idea. Most change efforts do not fail because the idea is wrong. They fail because leaders skip steps, or declare victory too early. Kotter's eight steps are a sequence for building the momentum and the conditions that make a change stick. The first four create the climate for change, the next two engage and enable people, and the last two implement and sustain.

  1. 1Establish a sense of urgency — make the case for why the status quo is costly
  2. 2Create a guiding coalition — assemble a group with enough credibility and power to lead
  3. 3Develop a vision and strategy — describe the future in a way people can picture
  4. 4Communicate the change vision — repeat it through every channel until it is understood
  5. 5Empower broad-based action — remove obstacles such as structures, skills and habits
  6. 6Generate short-term wins — plan visible early improvements and celebrate them
  7. 7Consolidate gains and produce more change — use early credibility to tackle the next layer
  8. 8Anchor new approaches in the culture — tie the change to how people are hired, rewarded and led

B. Why it is one of the most important frameworks for a manager

Problems it solves. New systems that nobody uses, resistance from people who were never brought along, and initiatives that start strongly and fade. Senior leaders use steps 1–4 to set urgency and direction. Middle managers carry the weight of steps 5–6, removing the obstacles that stop their teams acting. Frontline teams supply the short-term wins.

What a manager misses without it. Technology or process change that is delivered but not adopted. The project is "complete", yet behaviour has not changed, because nobody built the urgency, the coalition or the habits that make the new way the normal way.

Limitations. The steps are presented as a sequence, but real change is messier and steps often overlap or loop back. The model is top-down, suits large-scale change better than small tweaks, and has been criticised as prescriptive with a thin empirical evidence base. It should not be used for routine improvements, or in a crisis where decisions must be taken faster than a coalition can be built.

Why it earns its place. Digital and AI programmes are now the most common reason a manager has to lead change. The technology is rarely the hard part. Kotter gives a checklist for the human side that is simple enough to use on a real programme plan.

C. Real-life managerial case

Contributor: Pratap Nayak · Making a knowledge system and Copilot agent stick

  1. 1Context — the same global cutting-tool manufacturer's engineering centre. I was the manager and the designer of a knowledge-management system and a Copilot agent built on the Microsoft 365 platform.
  2. 2The challenge — expert knowledge sat with individuals and took long to find. The decision was how to capture it, and how to get it used first by the technology function and then by sales, design and application engineering globally. If it was not adopted, the investment would simply sit unused.
  3. 3What happened — the system captured more than 1,000 knowledge assets. Solution turnaround became about 50% faster and the time to find knowledge fell by about 90%. Because it worked for the technology function, it was redesigned to serve other functions, and it was recognised with a company strategic award.
  4. 4Applying the framework — mapped against Kotter's steps. Urgency was visible in slow knowledge discovery (step 1). The early speed and time-saving results were the short-term wins (step 6), and the extension to other functions was consolidating gains and producing more change (step 7). The steps in between, a named guiding coalition (step 2) and communication of a shared vision (steps 3–4), are where the framework asks a leader to be most deliberate before scaling.
  5. 5What would change — next time I would name a cross-functional guiding coalition before expanding beyond the first function, so that sales and design leaders co-own the vision rather than receive it. I would also anchor the change in onboarding and performance routines (step 8), so use does not depend on enthusiasm.
  6. 6Lesson — a tool is not a transformation. Plan for adoption as carefully as for capability, and turn early wins into the credibility for the next stage.

3 · Situational Leadership

A. Explanation of the framework

Origin. Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard introduced the model in 1969 as the life-cycle theory of leadership, published in Management of Organizational Behavior (Hersey & Blanchard, 1969). Blanchard and colleagues later refined it as Situational Leadership II (Blanchard, Zigarmi & Zigarmi, 1985).

Core idea. There is no single best leadership style. An effective leader adjusts how much direction and how much support to give, according to the follower's competence and commitment on the specific task at hand. The same person can need different styles for different tasks, and the right style changes as they develop.

The model combines two behaviours, directive (telling what, how and when) and supportive (listening, encouraging, involving). They give four styles, each matched to a development level:

  1. S1Directing (high directive, low support) — for the enthusiastic beginner who lacks skill but is keen
  2. S2Coaching (high directive, high support) — for the disillusioned learner who has some skill but is losing confidence
  3. S3Supporting (low directive, high support) — for the capable but cautious performer whose skill is high but confidence varies
  4. S4Delegating (low directive, low support) — for the self-reliant achiever who is both skilled and committed

Hersey and Blanchard's earlier labels were telling, selling, participating and delegating. The practical skill is diagnosis: judge the development level honestly, then flex the style.

B. Why it is one of the most important frameworks for a manager

Problems it solves. Micromanaging experts, abandoning beginners, uneven onboarding, stalled delegation and weak succession planning. Frontline team leaders use it to get new people productive. Middle managers use it to build capability across a team. Senior leaders use it to develop their own successors and to avoid one leadership style for every situation.

What a manager misses without it. A single default style. Over-directing skilled people drains their motivation and slows the team. Under-directing beginners produces errors, anxiety and quiet disengagement. Both look like "performance problems" when the cause is a mismatch of style.

Limitations. It depends on the manager diagnosing development level accurately, which is easy to get wrong. Empirical support for the model is mixed. It focuses on the leader-follower pair and says little about team dynamics, culture or organisational context. In a genuine emergency, directing is needed whatever the person's skill level.

Why it earns its place. Every manager is judged, in the end, by the people they develop. The model gives a simple, repeatable question to ask before each delegation or coaching conversation: what does this person need from me on this task, right now? That question alone prevents many avoidable conflicts and retention problems, and it scales from a first-line team to a senior leadership group.

C. Real-life managerial case

Contributor: Pratap Nayak · Building a team and growing its people

  1. 1Context — a global manufacturer's engineering centre where I established the solutions engineering team from the ground up. As technical manager I led engineers with very different levels of experience.
  2. 2The challenge — build technical capability across the team and build a leadership pipeline, while keeping people engaged. The stake was the team's ability to deliver globally and to retain the people it had trained.
  3. 3What happened — I introduced a skill matrix, which showed a skill gap for about half of the team. We then ran talent development initiatives built on performance assessment and competency frameworks, and upskilled all team members. Two associate engineers were developed into assistant managers, and the team recorded zero attrition.
  4. 4Applying the framework — the skill matrix was, in effect, a diagnosis of development level, task by task. Read through Situational Leadership: direct the newcomer on a new competency (S1), coach once skills grow but confidence dips (S2), support the capable but cautious engineer (S3), and delegate real ownership to the self-reliant achiever (S4). The two engineers who became assistant managers show the full path from S1 to S4 on management tasks.
  5. 5What would change — the matrix identified gaps in skill. Situational Leadership adds the second half: a stated leadership style per person and per task, reviewed regularly. That would stop me over-directing experienced engineers, and would make the shift from coaching to delegating deliberate rather than intuitive. It would also give new managers a shared vocabulary for talking about how they are being led.
  6. 6Lesson — diagnose before you decide how much to direct. Development level belongs to a person on a task, not to a person overall. Leading a team is a series of small, task-level calls, and the best ones are made on purpose.

4 · The Balanced Scorecard

A. Explanation of the framework

Origin. Robert Kaplan and David Norton introduced the Balanced Scorecard in their 1992 Harvard Business Review article "The Balanced Scorecard: Measures That Drive Performance", and developed it into a strategy-management system in their 1996 book (Kaplan & Norton, 1992; 1996).

Core idea. Financial results tell you what has already happened, so managing by them alone is like driving by the rear-view mirror. The Scorecard balances financial measures with the drivers of future results, all derived from the organisation's vision and strategy. For each of four perspectives, a manager defines objectives, measures, targets and initiatives.

  1. 1Financial — how do we look to owners? Revenue, cost, profitability
  2. 2Customer — how do customers see us? Delivery, quality, responsiveness, satisfaction
  3. 3Internal business processes — what must we excel at? Cycle time, rework, throughput
  4. 4Learning and growth — can we keep improving? Skills, knowledge, systems, culture

The perspectives form a chain of cause and effect. Capability in people and systems improves internal processes, better processes serve customers better, and satisfied customers produce financial results. A good scorecard tells that story with a few measures per perspective, not a long list. Kaplan and Norton later added strategy maps (2000, 2004), which draw those cause-and-effect links explicitly from objective to objective.

B. Why it is one of the most important frameworks for a manager

Problems it solves. Managers drowning in metrics, KPIs that do not link to strategy, short-term thinking, and strategy that never reaches daily work. Senior leaders use it to translate strategy into objectives. Middle managers cascade it into team measures. Frontline staff gain a line of sight from their work to the organisation's goals.

What a manager misses without it. Optimising one measure at the expense of the others, for example faster delivery at the cost of quality or team capability. A technical manager also struggles to explain value in the language the business uses, which is exactly the gap between technical results and business strategy.

Limitations. Designing a good scorecard takes effort and agreement on strategy. It can degrade into a long KPI list, measures can be gamed, and the cause-and-effect links between perspectives are assumed rather than proven. It goes stale if strategy changes and the scorecard does not. It is poorly suited to small teams without a clear strategy, or to fast-moving situations where measures cannot be defined in advance.

Why it earns its place. It is the framework that most directly connects technical delivery to business strategy, which is the bridge a senior role depends on. It also gives a manager a structured way to defend a budget or a decision with evidence from all four perspectives.

C. Real-life managerial case

Contributor: Pratap Nayak · Making an engineering centre's performance visible

  1. 1Context — the global cutting-tool manufacturer's engineering centre, handling around 1,300 customer projects across many industries. I was the manager responsible for its delivery performance.
  2. 2The challenge — delivery data lived in project databases, and decision-makers needed a timely, trustworthy view of performance. The decision was what to show, and how, so that leaders could act on it and see the centre's value.
  3. 3What happened — I built interactive Power BI dashboards by integrating our project databases, supporting data-driven decisions. Over the years the centre reached record on-time delivery of about 98%, a record lead time of 5 days, productivity gains of about 25% in an earlier role, and project work that generated revenue of around USD 20 million.
  4. 4Applying the framework — the Balanced Scorecard would organise those measures by strategy rather than by what the data allowed. Financial: revenue generated and annual cost saving. Customer: on-time delivery and lead time. Internal processes: revision hours and first-time-right. Learning and growth: skill-matrix coverage, the share of the team upskilled, and knowledge assets captured.
  5. 5What would change — the dashboards showed the numbers. A scorecard adds the causal story from capability to process to customer to financial result, and it guards against pushing lead time down at the cost of quality or the team's development. It would also have made the case to senior stakeholders in business terms, and given each measure an owner and a target tied to strategy.
  6. 6Lesson — measures need a story. Structure them around strategy, not around data availability, and keep the leading indicators next to the lagging ones. A dashboard answers what happened, while a scorecard also answers why it matters and what to do next. I would use it to brief senior stakeholders each quarter.

D. A published Indian example

Tata Steel's use of the Balanced Scorecard is documented in George Joseph's (2009) management accounting case, “Mapping, measurement and alignment of strategy using the balanced scorecard: The Tata Steel case” (Accounting Education, 18(2), 117–130). The case draws on interviews, internal company documents and published sources, and examines how an Indian steel company used the scorecard for strategic execution, with particular attention to the strategy map and measurement described above.

How the Four Connect

The Scorecard sets what matters and how to tell. DMAIC improves the process behind a measure that is off target. Kotter's steps make sure the improved way of working is adopted and kept. Situational Leadership shapes how I lead each person through the change. The result is then checked against the scorecard, which starts the next cycle. Used together, they cover the strategy, the process, the organisation and the person.
