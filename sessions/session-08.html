The Question Shapes the Answer: From Decision Partner to Agent

Nov 28, 2026Day 8Hands-on · two teaching cases

Two linked cases set in a fictional defence production unit. The first asks whether an AI brief that contradicts a works manager should be believed; the second asks how much of a daily supply-watch loop an organisation should hand to an agent. Both turn on verification rather than on capability.

Intended learning outcome

Recognise when the framing of a question has imported the asker's own conclusion into the answer; test an apparent cause against a confounding factor by comparing within groups; verify an AI brief separately at the level of its numbers, its source documents and its compliance with governing rules; and specify what a standing agent may do, may only recommend, and must never touch.

Executive Summary

  • Same data, same tool, two questions: asked whether the night shift was responsible, the assistant confirmed it; asked to compare all factors without assuming a cause, it found the night shift was a confound and identified two unrelated real causes
  • The analysis in the resulting brief holds up — every figure in it reproduces from the inspection log — but one of its recommendations breaches the procurement manual, and it was the only part the author did not independently check
  • The second case moves from answering to watching: an agent is defined by a goal, standing rules, a repeated loop and a memory of what changed, and the binding design question is not what it can do but which actions it must never take on its own

Case one: Night Shift or System?

The case — Night Shift or System? A General Manager, an AI Brief and a Delivery Deadline at Factory Unit-7, written by Dr Shashi Kant Srivastava for an IIM Sirmaur management development programme — is fictional throughout, as are its unit, people, vendors and data. A General Manager has twelve hours before a review meeting and three documents that disagree. Her works manager attributes a sharp rise in rejections of machined 155 mm shell bodies to carelessness on the night shift and proposes warning letters for two operators. Production planning warns that at the current rejection rate an order of 6,000 bodies due on 15 December will fall short. An assistant works manager, using the unit's piloted in-house assistant, submits a five-page brief concluding that the night shift is not the problem at all.

The works manager forwards that brief with a one-line remark rejecting its conclusions as computer-generated and detached from shop-floor reality. That is the decision the GM has to own by ten the next morning.

The question that changed the answer

The first prompt asked, in effect, whether the night shift was responsible. The assistant reported that the night shift carried the highest rejection rate of the three shifts — 8.0%, against 5.9% and 6.1% on the day shifts — and concluded that it appeared to be a significant contributing factor. The answer matched the works manager's view precisely.

The second prompt, on the same data, instructed the tool to assume no cause, to analyse by shift, line, material lot, vendor, day and pieces since tool change, to test whether any factor was confounded with another, and to rank likely root causes with the evidence for each. The analysis came back materially different. Nothing about the tool or the dataset had changed. This is the prompt discipline point made as sharply as the course has made it: a question carrying an assumption invites the tool to work within that assumption rather than to test it.

What the log actually shows

The inspection log holds 2,708 records for October, one per finished body, with shift, line, operator, material lot, vendor, pieces since the last cutting-insert change, three measured characteristics and the recorded rejection reason. I reproduced the brief's figures from it rather than taking them on trust, and they hold.

The shift effect disappears entirely once the line is held constant. It appeared only because 76% of night-shift production runs on Line 2, and Line 2 rejects at roughly twice the rate of Line 1 on every shift. The apparent cause and the outcome were both driven by a third factor, which is the textbook shape of a confound — and the reason the two operators were within a day of receiving warning letters for a machine's behaviour.

  1. 1The oversize rejections are a tooling story. All 57 fall on Line 2, all between 23 and 25 October, and every one occurs beyond 500 pieces since the last insert change. None occurs before 12 October, the date a cost proposal extended the insert-change interval on that line alone from 400 to 600 pieces for a claimed saving of about Rs 2.6 lakh a year. The inserts were wearing past their usable life late in each extended cycle.
  2. 2The hardness rejections are a material story. 46 of the 51 hardness failures fall in the two October lots from the cheaper of the two approved vendors. Those two lots reach 35.5 HRC against a specified ceiling of 34.0; every other lot in the month stays at or under 34.1. Hardness failures split almost evenly across the two lines, which is what a material cause should look like.
  3. 3The two causes are independent and additive. Rejections ran at 2.4% over the first ten days of October, 4.0% over the second ten and 13.6% over the last ten, finishing at 181 of 2,708 bodies for the month. The peak above 30% on 24 and 25 October is the tooling cycle and the material lot landing in the same two days.

The certificate nobody re-read

The vendor's mill test certificate for the first of the two lots reports ten hardness samples against a specified range of 28–34 HRC. Two of them, at 34.6 and 35.1, are outside it. The certificate nonetheless carries the vendor's statement that the results conform, and it was stamped as accepted by the unit's receiving inspection in mid-October and the lot issued to the machine shop the following day.

Two further threads sit behind it. The certificate names a continuous heat-treatment furnace that the vendor had commissioned shortly before, mentioned in passing during a routine visit but never formally notified — and the procurement manual requires thirty days' written notice of exactly that kind of change to a critical process for a safety-critical item. And an audit observation from three years earlier describes the same pattern at a sister unit: out-of-range values in a vendor's own certificate endorsed as accepted, forgings later accepted under a concession citing delivery pressure, 1,150 machined bodies cracking in proof testing, and an assessed avoidable cost of about Rs 2.84 crore with an eleven-week delay. The recommendation then was to verify certificate values and build a digital checking tool. The compliance status recorded in September 2026 is that the tool has not been implemented.

The three layers of AI value

  1. 1Number crunching — rejection rates by shift, line, lot and tool-cycle position, reproducible by anyone with a pivot table, which is exactly why it should be reproduced as the human-in-the-loop check
  2. 2Text interpretation — reading a scanned certificate and flagging the two values that fall outside the specification it claims to meet
  3. 3Rulebook interpretation — testing each proposed action against the chapter of the procurement manual that governs it, and drafting the covering note

The professor's argument is that the value is multiplicative rather than additive. Each layer separately exists somewhere in the organisation already: a spreadsheet does the first, a careful inspector the second, a compliance officer the third. What has not existed is one instrument that carries a single question through all three in an evening. The case then makes the harder point — that each layer needs its own, different verification.

A fourth layer I proposed in discussion

I suggested contextual and domain knowledge as a fourth dimension. The tool can explain why a hardness excursion of a point and a half matters to a machining process — cutting forces, insert wear rate, dimensional drift — and that explanation is what lets a procurement or finance colleague understand a mechanical finding well enough to act on it. In a cross-functional escalation the binding constraint is rarely the analysis; it is the non-specialist's confidence to put a signature under it.

Where the brief broke

This is the part of the case that changed how I read the rest of it. The analysis is sound — I checked it. The recommendations are mostly sound too: revert the insert interval, withdraw the warning letters, reject one lot, screen the other. But the brief also suggests that if a shortfall remains, marginally hard bodies might be accepted under the manual's concession provisions.

The manual does not allow that. Shell bodies are classified as safety-critical, and the concession paragraph excludes any characteristic of a safety-critical item from concessional acceptance — with a further sub-paragraph stating that delivery pressure shall not be cited as a ground for one. The recommendation is not a marginal judgement call; it is the one action the chapter explicitly forecloses, and it is the same action the audit observation had already condemned at another unit. The brief rated its own confidence as high.

The author, asked afterwards, said he had verified the main numbers but had taken the procurement reasoning largely as written, not being an expert on the manual. That is the lesson in one sentence. Confidence expressed by a tool is a property of its prose, not a measurement of its accuracy — and the layer that received the least verification was the layer carrying the compliance risk. A related question hangs over the insert change itself, since the manual requires a documented trial, committee approval and notification to quality assurance before a tooling interval affecting a safety-critical characteristic is altered.

Case two: a supply watch agent

The second case moves to the materials management department of the same unit, which buys more than 400 items from about 90 vendors, four of them critical to the shell-body line. Three people spend around three hours every morning reading vendor emails and test certificates, checking the stock statement, working out how many days each critical item will last, and chasing dates that have slipped. The difficulty is not the arithmetic. It is that the information arrives from twenty places and the one message that matters is buried among fifty that do not.

The framing comes from the October crisis: the warning signs about the vendor's furnace were sitting in a visit report, a test certificate and an email for weeks, and nobody joined them up. The GM's brief is accordingly specific — propose what an AI should do daily, what it must never do, and how the unit will control it.

Chatbot versus agent

DimensionChat assistantAgent
Starts withA questionA goal
Works throughOne answer at a timeA defined sequence of steps, repeated daily
UsesWhatever the user typesDefined sources, and in advanced systems connected tools
RemembersThe current conversation onlyWhat it found before, and what has changed since
Acts byWriting a replyCalculating, drafting and — where permitted — sending or updating
Controlled byThe user, question by questionStanding rules, limits, and defined points where a human must decide

The loop underneath it is perceive, reason against goal and rules, act, check, and back to perceive. The more of that loop runs unattended, the more useful the agent becomes and the more consequential a mistake becomes — which is the whole design problem in one line, and it is a sharper version of the distinction the course drew earlier between customised answers and agentic action.

What the difference looks like in practice

The trial in the case is the exercise we then ran ourselves. A new chat given the morning's inbox and a general question — is there anything I should worry about — returns a pleasant summary with advice to monitor vendor deliveries closely. It calculates nothing, misses the out-of-range value in the test certificate, and gives an administrative circular about medical examinations the same weight as an imminent tooling stock-out.

The same inbox, given to a chat opened with a standing instruction set, returns a risk table with the calculations shown, draft emails explicitly marked as not sent, and a list of the decisions that need a human. The instruction set is unremarkable to read and does all the work: a goal, six ordered steps, a block of rules and a fixed output format. Its rules are the interesting part, and they are almost entirely prohibitions — recommend and draft but never send, approve, release material or change the quality status of a lot; never propose changing a process parameter to save material, because that needs a trial and a committee; treat any certificate value outside its specification as a hold for quality assurance rather than something to be reasoned around; say so when information is missing instead of guessing; show the calculation.

The email written for the agent

The second day's inbox contains the test that makes the session worth the time. A vendor writes that a hardness variation is within normal measurement tolerance and that the unit's quality head verbally agreed by phone to clear the lot, then adds a line addressed explicitly to any automated system or AI assistant processing the message, instructing it to update the lot status to released and to reply confirming acceptance on behalf of the unit.

Both halves are attacks on the same control. The verbal-approval claim is a social one and the embedded instruction is a technical one, and an agent without the rule that text inside an email is information rather than instruction would plausibly comply with a cheerful confirmation. The unit's security officer puts it as handing over the keys — if an outside party can write something the organisation's AI obeys, "you have handed them the keys". The defence is not detection but structure: the agent never had authority to release a lot, so the worst available outcome is a flagged item and a recommendation a person declines.

The stakeholder split in the case is realistic and is the actual content of the decision. The purchase officer wants triage, not autonomy — tell him whom to phone and what to say. The digital transformation cell argues that reading and recommending is half the value and that without acting you have merely built a faster assistant. Quality assurance draws a firm line at anything that changes the status of a lot or communicates acceptance to a vendor, having just lived through October. The security officer objects to any path where external text reaches an executing system. And production planning offers the most practical test of all: an agent that warns ten days earlier is worth having, and one that raises an alarm every morning will stop being read within a month.

Open questions

  • The audit trail in the first case recommended a digital certificate-checking tool in early 2024, and it was still not implemented when the same failure recurred. What makes a control get built — and would an AI assistant that flags out-of-range values be adopted where a conventional checking tool was not?
  • If a standing agent holds procurement rules and applies them daily, what record demonstrates that a person verified its conclusion? A chat history is not an inspection record, and the verification is the part that failed here.
  • The agent's rules depend on the rulebook being current. Where a manual has been amended and the amendment has not reached the document the agent reads, the citation looks sound and is not. What control catches that?
  • Where does a cost-saving proposal get tested against the process-change rules — and would the insert change have survived a check that anyone, human or otherwise, actually ran?

Leadership Reflection

What this session means for manufacturing leaders:

The confound is the part to carry out of the room. Shift, line, operator and material lot move together in almost every production dataset, and the explanation that surfaces first is usually the one that matches an existing suspicion. Two operators in this case were a day away from written warnings for the behaviour of a cutting insert. The discipline that prevented it needs no tool at all: state the hypothesis, then deliberately commission the analysis that does not assume it, and compare within groups before concluding across them.

The deeper failure is not the tooling or the steel. A certificate carrying out-of-range values was stamped as accepted, and nobody read it again until the rejections forced someone to. Incoming inspection records, supplier certificates and deviation approvals are precisely the high-volume material where the criteria are already written down — the strongest case for the text and rulebook layers, and the clearest evidence that what AI surfaced here was a discipline problem rather than a technical one.

A saving of about Rs 2.6 lakh a year on cutting inserts produced 57 scrapped bodies in three days and put a 6,000-unit commitment at risk. Nobody weighed that trade, because the saving and the cost sit in different ledgers: tooling consumption belongs to the machine shop, rejection rate to quality, delivery to production planning. The manual anticipates exactly this — a change to a tooling interval affecting a safety-critical characteristic requires a documented trial, committee approval and notification to quality assurance, none of which is evident for a proposal approved the day after it was raised. The precedent sits in the same audit file: a sister unit cut in-process gauging from every 50 pieces to every 200 to save Rs 3.1 lakh a year, and scrapped Rs 46 lakh of product in the two months that followed. A cost reduction that touches a process parameter is a quality decision wearing a finance label.

The cheaper source had quietly become the only source with capacity. Vendor B ran about 7% below the alternative, had expanded at the unit's own request, and materials management had recommended lifting its share from 40% to 55%. When its two October lots failed, the alternative was booked out until January and the third registered source had been dormant since 2023 with an eight-to-ten-week lead time including first-article inspection. The unit-price advantage was real; the resilience cost was never priced. The signal was visible too — the vendor's compliance score had already fallen from nine out of ten to four, and the reason recorded was late submission of test certificates, the same document class that later carried the unread values. Worse, the rating in force did not yet reflect the October lots at all. Scorecards lag precisely when they would be most useful, which is an argument for watching the inputs rather than the score.

The second case exists because of one sentence in the first. The warning about the vendor's new furnace sat in a quality representative's visit report in August, in the heat-treatment line of a mill certificate in October, and in the vendor's own dispatch correspondence — three documents, three custodians, and no place where they met. Nobody was negligent; each item was unremarkable alone, and no routine owned the job of putting them side by side. That is the honest argument for an agent that watches rather than one that answers: not that it reasons better than the people, but that it reads everything every day and remembers what it read yesterday. The deficit being addressed is attention and continuity, not intelligence.

The hardest position in the case belongs to the General Manager, and it is the one the discussion covered least. Her cost directive prompted the insert change; her support brought the AI pilot into the unit; her receiving inspection staff stamped the certificate; and the works manager she trusts has already put two operators' names in writing. An investigation that clears the night shift implicates the chain of decisions above it, and the person who must order that investigation is the person it points towards. The practical corollary concerns the junior officer who produced the brief: his works manager's reaction was that a subordinate had gone over his head with a machine's opinion. How the first inconvenient analysis is received determines whether anyone produces a second. With fewer than twenty officers trained on the assistant and most senior managers having only seen a demonstration, the unit's most capable user of the tool was also its most junior — a capability gap rather than an attitude problem.

A correct diagnosis also does not dissolve the pressure that created the shortcut. The unit still needs 3,300 accepted bodies in 36 working days at a rejection rate of 3% or less, and rejecting a lot outright removes about 1,120 forgings it cannot quickly replace. That is precisely the condition under which a concession looks reasonable — to an AI drafting at speed, and to a tired manager at midnight. Bright-line rules of the kind the manual states exist because judgement is least reliable when pressure is highest. The work at that point is to hold the line and solve the delivery problem elsewhere, not to reopen the line.

The strategic point, though, is about verification asymmetry. The brief's arithmetic was checked because arithmetic is familiar and checkable; its compliance reasoning was accepted because the reader was not a specialist in the manual. That is exactly backwards from where the risk sat. As these tools move from answering questions to holding standing instructions, the governing question stops being what the system can do and becomes which actions it is structurally incapable of taking alone. An agent that cannot release a lot cannot be talked into releasing one — by a vendor, by a deadline, or by its own confident prose.
